The basics

How your engine
makes power

Every engine is an air pump. The more air it can take in, the more fuel it can burn and the more power it makes. A turbo forces in extra air, and the ECU decides how much.

1Air in

Air passes the filter. Sensors measure how much is coming in and how warm it is, and report to the ECU, the car's computer.

2Squeezed and cooled

The turbo's compressor packs the air in: that's boost. Squeezing heats the air, so the intercooler cools it again. Cooler air is denser and safer.

3Burned and reused

The ECU adds fuel and a spark (a diesel ignites from compression alone). The exhaust then spins the turbo's turbine, which drives the compressor on the same shaft.

2 LEVERS

Software decides how hard the engine works. Hardware decides how much air and fuel it can flow. Stage 1 uses the first lever; Stages 2 and 3 add the second.

The basics

Inside the ECU

The engine control unit is a small, rugged computer. Hundreds of times a second it reads the sensors, looks up its maps and sets the boost, fuel and spark. Tuning means rewriting those maps.

  1. CoverSeals out water and dust, and spreads heat away from the electronics.
  2. Circuit boardPower supply and the drivers that fire the injectors and coils and move the throttle and wastegate.
  3. Processor and flash memoryThe program and its maps live here. This is what we read, change and write back.
  4. ConnectorLinks the ECU to every sensor and actuator on the car.
  5. Base and heat sinkHolds the board and carries heat into the car's body.

Why factories leave power on the table

  • One engine is sold in many countries, so it's set up for the worst fuel and hottest weather it might meet.
  • Carmakers often sell one engine at two or three power levels. The software sets the difference.
  • Settings favour emissions tests, noise limits and long service intervals over response.

What we never touch

  • Emissions systems: catalyst, DPF, EGR, AdBlue.
  • The immobiliser and anti-theft systems.
  • Safety systems: ABS, airbags, stability control.
  • The odometer.

The basics

What a tune actually changes

Inside the ECU, most decisions come from tables called maps. Each one is a grid: engine speed along one side, load or pedal along the other, and a value in every cell.

Example boost maps for a turbo petrol engine (an illustration). Darker cells ask for more boost. Stage 1 raises the target in the mid-range, where you feel it most, and stays gentle at low load so the car is just as smooth in traffic.

Boost target

How much the turbo pushes, at each speed and load. Kept within the turbo's safe speed.

Fuel

Petrol: the air-fuel ratio. Diesel: injection quantity, timing and rail pressure.

Ignition timing

When the spark fires (petrol). Optimised for your fuel, with knock control fully active.

Torque limits

The ceiling the ECU allows, often per gear. Raised to match, within the gearbox's capacity.

Pedal map

How much the throttle opens for your foot. Made smooth and progressive, never jumpy.

Protection

Limits for temperature, knock and smoke. We keep them all, and often tighten them.

A good tune changes hundreds of values across many maps. It's not one "power slider", and that's why every file is built for one car and checked with logs.

Stage 2 · Part 1How it's fitted: page

The intercooler

Squeezing air heats it up. The intercooler is a radiator for the air the turbo sends to your engine, and at Stage 2 it's the most important part.

  1. Inlet end tankSpreads hot air from the turbo across the core.
  2. Bar-and-plate coreThe charge air flows through these sealed passages.
  3. FinsOutside air flows across them and carries the heat away.
  4. Outlet end tankCollects the cooled air and sends it to the throttle.
  5. Couplers and clampsMulti-ply silicone joints that hold the higher boost.

Why it's fitted

Factory intercoolers are sized for factory boost. After a few hard runs, or on a 40 °C day, they heat-soak. Intake temperature climbs and the ECU pulls timing to protect the engine, so power fades just when you need it.

What it does

A larger core with more frontal area and internal fins keeps intake temperature low and steady, run after run. Cooler air is denser, carrying more oxygen, and far less likely to knock.

What we check

  • Intake temperature on back-to-back runs
  • Boost before and after the core
  • Smoke test for leaks
  • Clearance and secure clamps
3%

Every 10 °C of cooler intake air makes the charge about 3% denser, so there's more oxygen to burn with every stroke.

Stage 2 · Part 2How it's fitted: page

Intake, boost pipes and valves

The engine's first breath. A freer intake reduces restriction before the turbo, and stronger pipes hold the extra boost after it.

  1. Airbox lidThe factory airbox stays: it's designed to draw cool air.
  2. High-flow panel filterWashable cotton gauze. Flows more air than paper while still filtering dust.
  3. Airbox base and snorkelTakes in outside air, away from engine-bay heat.
  4. Silicone intake hoseSmooth bore, no collapsing under suction.
  5. Stainless clampsHold every joint tight under boost.

Why it's fitted

Any restriction before the turbo makes it spin harder for the same boost, which means hotter air and more turbo stress. We prefer a high-flow filter in the factory box. Open cone filters often suck in hot air, and in the monsoon they can suck in water.

Boost pipes

Factory plastic and rubber pipes can balloon or split at higher boost. Reinforced silicone hoses with proper clamps hold the pressure. The diverter valve releases pressure when you lift off; an uprated piston valve stops it leaking at higher boost.

What we check

  • Air-mass and pressure readings after fitting
  • Fuel trims stay close to zero
  • Boost leak test
  • The calibration is matched to the new parts

Stage 2 · Part 3How it's fitted: page

Downpipe and high-flow catalyst

The first section of exhaust after the turbo. On many turbo engines it's the biggest restriction, and freeing it wakes the turbo up.

  1. Turbo outlet flangeMachined to seal perfectly against the turbo.
  2. Wide, smooth bendsMandrel-bent stainless steel keeps the full diameter through every bend.
  3. High-flow catalytic converterBigger cells for freer flow, and it still cleans the exhaust.
  4. Joint to the exhaustConnects to the rest of the factory system.
  5. Lambda sensor bossesBoth oxygen sensors stay, before and after the catalyst.

Why it's fitted

A turbo is driven by the pressure difference across its turbine. Lower pressure after it means the turbo spools sooner and runs cooler, so the same boost costs the engine less work.

What we fit

A stainless downpipe with a high-flow sports catalyst. The second lambda sensor keeps checking that the catalyst works, just like the factory one.

What we check

  • Lambda readings either side of the catalyst
  • No exhaust leaks or fault codes
  • Heat clearance from hoses and wiring
  • Sound at idle and at cruise

What we don't do: we don't fit "decat" pipes or remove catalytic converters. A car without a working catalyst pollutes far more and won't pass an emissions check.

Stage 2 · Part 4How it's fitted: page

Spark plugs and ignition

More boost means higher pressure and heat in the cylinder. The spark has to stay strong, and the plug's tip has to stay cool.

Why one step colder

A plug's heat range is how quickly it passes heat from its tip into the cylinder head. With more boost, a standard plug's tip can get hot enough to light the mixture early, before the spark. That's pre-ignition, and it can break a piston. A colder plug sheds heat faster and stays safe.

Gap and coils

We set a slightly smaller gap so high boost can't blow the spark out, and we check the logs for misfires. A weak coil shows up quickly on a tuned car and gets replaced.

How often

Tuned turbo engines need plugs more often than the handbook says. We'll give you an interval for your engine.

Automatic and dual-clutch gearboxes

Gearbox (TCU) tuning

Many automatics limit engine torque to protect themselves, and shift early for economy. A TCU tune raises those limits within the gearbox's real capacity and, on dual-clutch boxes (DCT), raises clutch clamping pressure so the clutches can't slip.

  • Firmer, quicker shifts when you drive hard; smooth in traffic.
  • Kick-down and shift points matched to the new power band.
  • Some gearboxes, such as dry-clutch DCTs, have firm limits. We'll tell you the safe ceiling.

Stage 2 · Diesel

Stage 2 for diesels

A diesel has no throttle and no spark. Its power comes from how much fuel is injected, and the turbo must supply enough air to burn that fuel cleanly.

Most modern diesels, from the Fortuner to the Scorpio-N, use a variable-geometry turbo (VGT). Movable vanes change how the exhaust hits the turbine, giving quick boost low down and plenty of flow at high rpm. The ECU controls the vanes, so the tune controls them too.

What's in a diesel Stage 2

  • Larger intercooler. The biggest gain: diesels heat-soak when towing or climbing ghats.
  • Intake and silicone boost pipes, as on page .
  • Clutch check, and an upgrade for high-torque manuals such as the Thar and Scorpio.
  • A calibration matched to the new parts and logged on the road.

What we watch on diesels

  • Exhaust gas temperature
  • Smoke: lambda under full load
  • Rail pressure, target against actual
  • Boost and vane position
  • DPF soot load and pressure

DPF, EGR and AdBlue stay. They're legal requirements on BS6 cars, and removing them raises pollution. A healthy DPF doesn't hold back Stage 1 or Stage 2 power.

No black smoke. Smoke is unburnt fuel: wasted money and the sign of a poor tune. Ours shouldn't smoke under full load.

Stage 3 · Part 1How it's fitted: page

The turbocharger

A turbo is two fans on one shaft. Exhaust spins the turbine, the turbine spins the compressor, and the compressor packs air into the engine, at well over 150,000 rpm.

  1. Compressor housingCollects air from the wheel and turns its speed into pressure: boost.
  2. Compressor wheelMachined aluminium. Its size and blade shape decide how much air the turbo can move.
  3. BackplateCloses and seals the compressor side.
  4. Bearing housing (CHRA)The heart: bearings and seals, cooled by oil and, on many turbos, coolant.
  5. Shaft and turbine wheelOne piece, in heat-resistant alloy. It runs red-hot at full load.
  6. Turbine housingAims the exhaust at the turbine. Its size (A/R) sets quick spool against top-end power.
  7. Wastegate actuatorLets exhaust bypass the turbine. This is how the ECU controls boost.
  8. Oil feed and drainFresh oil in at the top, out at the bottom. Without it, a turbo dies in seconds.

Why turbos fail, and how we prevent it

  • Oil starvation or dirty oil: the right oil, changed on time.
  • Overspeeding: turbo speed is limited in every calibration.
  • Heat soak: let it idle briefly after hard driving.
  • Debris: a good filter, and a clean, sealed intake.

Stage 3 · Part 2

Hybrid or bigger turbo?

Stage 3 starts with the turbo, and the choice decides how your car will feel. It's a trade between how early boost arrives and how much power it makes at the top.

An illustration of how the power curve changes shape with each turbo. Not measured figures for a specific car.

Factory turbo

Spools quickly and suits Stages 1 and 2. Pushed beyond its design, it runs out of breath at high rpm and gets hot.

Hybrid turbo

The factory housings with a larger compressor wheel and upgraded turbine and bearings. It bolts on, boost arrives a little later, and there's much more top end. The best balance for road cars.

Bigger turbo

A larger frame turbo makes the most power but builds boost later, and needs more supporting parts. Best for dedicated project cars.

A new turbo means a new calibration. Boost, fuel, timing and turbo-speed limits are rebuilt from scratch for it, then proven with logs. Fuel, clutch and cooling come next: pages –.

Stage 3 · Part 3How it's fitted: page

The fuel system

More air needs more fuel. At Stage 3 the factory pump and injectors can reach their limit, and running lean is the fastest way to damage a turbo engine.

  1. High-pressure pumpDriven by the camshaft; raises fuel pressure for the injectors.
  2. Pump pistonIts size and stroke set how much fuel the pump delivers.
  3. FollowerRides on the cam lobe and pushes the piston.
  4. Cam lobeTurns the engine's rotation into pump strokes.
  5. Pressure control valveThe ECU sets the pressure it needs.
  1. ConnectorThe ECU opens the injector electrically.
  2. SealsHold the injector tight against rail and head.
  3. Injector bodyOpens for thousandths of a second at a time.
  4. NozzleFinely machined holes shape the spray.
  5. SprayA fine mist burns completely and cleanly.

The pressures

Petrol direct injection: about 200–350 bar. Diesel common rail: 1,600–2,500 bar, over a thousand times a tyre.

Why upgrade

If the pump can't hold its target at full load, the mixture goes lean: hot and prone to knock. An uprated pump and larger injectors supply the extra.

What we monitor

  • Rail pressure, target vs actual
  • Injector opening time
  • Lambda at full load

Stage 3 · Part 4How it's fitted: page

Clutch and flywheel

Every newton-metre goes through the clutch. Factory clutches have some margin. Stage 2 can use it up on a worn clutch, and Stage 3 always needs more.

  1. Release bearingPushes the diaphragm when you press the pedal.
  2. Pressure plateIts diaphragm spring clamps the disc. Clamping force decides how much torque it holds.
  3. Clutch discFriction lining on both faces, with damper springs to soften engagement.
  4. FlywheelOften dual-mass. Smooths engine pulses and provides the friction face.

Signs it's slipping

Revs rise but speed doesn't, usually in 4th or 5th at full throttle from low rpm. Slipping glazes the clutch quickly, so stop using full power and let us check it.

The upgrade

A pressure plate with higher clamping force and a stronger disc. Organic linings stay smooth for daily driving; ceramic or sintered linings hold more torque but grab harder.

Our advice

For most road cars: an uprated organic clutch, keeping the dual-mass flywheel where possible. A lighter single-mass flywheel is stronger but adds vibration and noise.

Stage 3 · Part 5

Cooling, oil and supporting parts

More power makes more heat, and more speed. These parts keep a tuned car reliable on long climbs, in city traffic and when you need to stop.

Radiator and fan

A higher-capacity radiator if coolant temperature creeps up on long climbs or in traffic.

Oil cooler

Keeps oil temperature stable. The oil protects the turbo's bearings, so its temperature matters.

Engine oil

Fully synthetic, to your engine's specification, changed sooner on hard-driven tuned cars.

Brakes

Uprated pads and fresh high-temperature fluid first; bigger discs and calipers for Stage 3.

Tyres

Quality tyres with the right load and speed rating. All your power goes through four small patches.

Mounts

Stiffer engine and gearbox mount inserts stop wheel hop and protect driveshafts, with a little more vibration.

Stopping comes first. If we think your brakes or tyres aren't up to the power you're asking for, we'll say so before the tune, not after.

Installation · Stage 3

Fitting the turbocharger

The most involved job in this book. Most turbo failures after a swap come from oil supply mistakes, so we take as much care over the oil as over the bolts.

Time 1–2 daysChecked by leak checks + boost and temperature logsPart page
  1. Disconnect the battery, drain the coolant and remove the heat shields.
  2. Remove the old turbo: intake, downpipe, oil and coolant lines, then the manifold nuts.
  3. Clean the flange faces; fit new gaskets and studs, and torque them in sequence.
  4. Fill the new turbo's bearing housing with fresh oil before connecting the feed.
  5. Fit new oil feed and drain lines with new washers. The drain must run downhill, with no kinks.
  6. Reconnect the downpipe, intake and coolant lines; refill and bleed the cooling system.
  7. Crank without starting until oil pressure builds, then idle and check every joint for leaks.
  8. Flash the Stage 3 calibration, then log boost, temperatures and turbo-speed limits.

Always replaced with a turbo: engine oil and filter, air filter, every gasket, stud and washer. If the old turbo failed, we also clean the intercooler and pipes, because debris or oil left inside can destroy the new one.

Fitting spark plugs

Installation · Stage 2

Fitting the downpipe

  1. Raise the car and let the exhaust cool completely.
  2. Unplug and remove both lambda sensors.
  3. Undo the turbo flange nuts and the rear joint; release the hangers.

Installation · Stage 3

Fitting the fuel pump and injectors

  1. Relieve the fuel pressure safely before opening anything.
  2. Disconnect the low- and high-pressure lines.
  3. Swap the pump, with a new follower and O-ring; torque the bolts evenly.
  4. Fit the injectors with new seals and combustion rings, and seat them evenly.
  5. Switch the ignition on to prime the system, and check every joint for leaks.
  6. Enter the new pump and injector data in the calibration, then log rail pressure.

Fuel

Ethanol and flex-fuel tuning

India is moving to ethanol-blended petrol (E20). Ethanol can be very good for a turbo engine, as long as the tune knows it's there.

Why ethanol helps

  • High octane. Pure ethanol rates around 108 RON, so it resists knock and allows more timing and boost.
  • Cooling. It absorbs a lot of heat as it evaporates, cooling the air in the cylinder.
  • Cleaner burn. Ethanol carries its own oxygen.

What it needs

  • More fuel. Ethanol carries about a third less energy than petrol, so higher blends need noticeably more fuel. E20 needs a few percent more; E85 needs about a third more.
  • A fuel system that keeps up (page ).
  • Compatible seals and hoses. Older cars weren't all built for E20. We check yours.

A tune can only be as healthy as the car it goes on. Before we tune, we check. And every file we write has safety limits built in.

Health check

  • Fault scan of every module, not just the engine
  • Live data: fuel trims, misfires, sensor readings
  • Battery and charging voltage
  • Plugs and coils (petrol) or injector corrections (diesel)
  • Boost leak test with smoke
  • Intake and exhaust leaks
  • Oil, coolant and belts
  • Clutch slip test on the road
  • Tyres and brakes, visual check
  • DPF condition (diesel)
  • Compression / leak-down (Stage 3, or on request)

Limits in every tune

  • Turbo speed kept within the maker's design range
  • Knock control fully active on every cylinder
  • Richer mixture at full load to protect pistons and catalyst
  • Exhaust temperature protection kept
  • Overboost protection with a safe fallback
  • Torque limited in low gears to protect the driveline
  • Power reduction on overheating kept active

After your tune

Looking after your tuned car

A tuned car isn't fragile, but it rewards good habits. These are the ones that matter most.

01Warm up, cool down

Don't use full power until the oil is warm, about 10 minutes of gentle driving. After hard driving, let the engine idle for 30–60 seconds before switching off.

02Use the right fuel

Use the fuel your tune was built for. If you need to use a lower grade, drive gently until the next fill-up, and tell us if you switch for good.

03Oil on time

Fully synthetic oil to your engine's specification. Changing it sooner than the handbook says is the cheapest protection your turbo can get.

04Plugs, coils and filter

Check plugs and coils at every service. Clean or replace the air filter often, especially in dusty months.

05Listen to the car

A warning light, smoke, a misfire or a sudden loss of power means: stop using full power and message us.

06Before a dealer visit

A software update at the service centre can overwrite your tune. Tell us before you go, and we'll advise.

07Tyres and brakes

Keep tyres at the right pressure and replace worn pads early. More power means you'll reach speed sooner, so you need to stop sooner too.

08Keep your record

Fill in the log on page at each service. It helps us, any mechanic, and the next owner if you sell.

Questions

Questions customers ask

Will tuning damage my engine?

A careful tune within the hardware's limits, on a healthy, well-maintained engine, is reliable. Problems come from aggressive generic files, poor maintenance or ignoring warning signs. That's why we check, log and set limits.

Will my mileage drop?

Driven the same way, most cars use about the same fuel, and some diesels use slightly less at a steady cruise. Use the extra power often and you'll use more.

Can I go back to stock?

Yes. We keep your original file and can flash it back at any time.

Can the service centre see it?

Sometimes, yes. And a dealer software update can overwrite the tune, so tell us before a service visit.

Which fuel should I use?

The one your tune was built for. Stage 1 can be made for regular fuel. Stage 2 and 3 petrol tunes usually need premium (95+ octane) or an ethanol blend.

Is it safe on an automatic?

Yes, with torque limits matched to the gearbox. On some automatics and DCTs we also recommend a gearbox (TCU) tune.

Will it be harder to drive in traffic?

No. Low-load areas stay close to stock, so the car is just as easy at walking pace. You'll only notice the difference when you press harder.

How long does it take?

Stage 1 usually takes a day, Stage 2 one to two days, and Stage 3 depends on parts.

Do I need Stage 1 before Stage 2?

No. Stage 2 comes with its own calibration, which replaces Stage 1. Many customers start with Stage 1 and add parts later.

My car has high mileage. Can I tune it?

Often, yes, after the health check. Worn clutches, weak coils or leaks get fixed first.

Do you use a dyno?

No. We tune and verify with road logs. Our figures are estimates for healthy, standard cars, and we say so.

Will it affect warranty or insurance?

It can. Please read page before you decide.

My car already has other parts. Can you tune it?

Yes. Tell us about every modification, including filters and exhausts, so the calibration matches the car.

What does it cost?

It depends on the car and the stage. Send us your model on WhatsApp for a free estimate.

Glossary

Tuning words, explained

A/RThe size ratio of a turbo housing. Smaller spools sooner; larger flows more at the top.

AFR / LambdaThe ratio of air to fuel. Lambda 1.0 is chemically exact; below 1.0 is richer.

BoostPressure above the atmosphere in the intake, made by the turbo. Measured in bar.

BS6India's current emission standard for new vehicles.

Catalytic converterTurns harmful exhaust gases into less harmful ones.

CHRAThe turbo's centre section: shaft, bearings and seals.

DCTDual-clutch transmission: an automatic with two clutches.

Diverter valveReleases boost pressure when you lift off the throttle.

DownpipeThe first section of exhaust after the turbo.

DPFDiesel particulate filter. Traps soot and burns it off.

ECUEngine control unit: the computer that runs the engine.

EGRExhaust gas recirculation. Feeds some exhaust back in to reduce NOx.

EGTExhaust gas temperature. Too high damages turbos and pistons.

FlashWriting a file to the ECU's memory.

Fuel trimThe ECU's small corrections to fuelling. Large trims point to a fault.

Heat rangeHow quickly a spark plug passes heat away from its tip.

Heat soakWhen parts get so hot they can't cool the air, and power fades.

hp / PSUnits of power: how fast the engine can do work.

Hybrid turboA factory turbo rebuilt with larger or better internals.

IntercoolerA radiator that cools the air after the turbo.

KnockUncontrolled burning that hammers the pistons. The ECU listens for it and retards the spark.

Lag / spoolHow long the turbo takes to build boost.

LogA recording of sensor data on a drive.

MAF / MAPSensors that measure air mass and manifold pressure.

MapA table in the ECU: for example, boost by rpm and load.

Nm (torque)Twisting force. It's what you feel as pull.

Octane (RON)A fuel's resistance to knock. Higher is safer under boost.

Pre-ignitionFuel lighting before the spark. It can destroy a piston.

Rail pressureFuel pressure at the injectors.

RemapChanging the ECU's maps: another word for a tune.

TCUTransmission control unit: the automatic gearbox's computer.

VGTVariable-geometry turbo, with movable vanes. Common on diesels.

WastegateA valve that lets exhaust bypass the turbine to control boost.
